Inclusive Education with AR

An AR learning experience built with a special education school.

01 · Overview

Bridging the gap
with spatial learning

Students with learning difficulties often engage better when information is presented visually and interactively. Traditional classroom tools rarely adapt to their needs, and this can create gaps in understanding or confidence.

Augmented reality felt like a natural way to bridge this. It brings learning into the real world and gives students a chance to interact with content in a way that feels more personal, more tactile and more intuitive.

Starting point

The question was not how to add technology to a classroom. It was how to use technology in ways that matched how these particular students already learn.

02 · Solution

Four needs,
one system

Working closely with a special education school, the teachers identified four areas where their students consistently struggled: life skills, social interaction, early reading support for learners with dyslexia, and phonics reinforcement. These became the four core learning paths in the application, because they reflected real classroom needs rather than theoretical assumptions.

With these needs in mind, I designed the experience to feel calm, predictable and friendly. The home screen leads students into short modules that use AR to bring everyday routines and academic concepts into their surroundings. Interactions are built around simple gestures and clear visual cues so that students can proceed without confusion.

Key constraint

The visual language uses soft colours and uncluttered layouts to support learners who are sensitive to overstimulation. Calm was a design requirement, not a visual preference.

03 · Architecture

Structure
before detail

Before addressing visual detail, I focused on validating the system's structure and technical foundations. The application follows a simple information architecture that branches from a single dashboard into the four learning paths. Each path contains short lessons supported by AR interactions.

To ensure smooth performance on devices with limited processing power, I evaluated several tracking methods and found that ORB offered the most consistent results across varying classroom conditions. Early prototypes used placeholder 3D objects to test spatial positioning, interaction flow and stability before introducing meaningful content.

Technical decision

Offline functionality and compatibility with older devices became essential constraints. Most classrooms cannot depend on strong internet connectivity, and the system had to work within those limits.

04 · Impact

What this
can change

Initial testing confirmed that the system was stable, engaging and accessible on hardware available in most schools. The next step is a controlled study with the partner school, focusing on measurable improvements in attention, comprehension, life skill confidence and teacher usability.

Working on this project taught me the value of creating AR experiences that feel grounded and supportive. It reinforced the need to consider accessibility from the start and showed me how small details, whether an animation or a gesture cue, can significantly impact a learner's experience. This project also led to a research paper later published in IEEE Xplore.

Broader lesson

This project has the potential to make learning more inclusive for students who are often underserved by traditional methods. It supports comprehension for learners with dyslexia, builds independence through repeatable life skill practice, and reduces the instructional burden on teachers.
