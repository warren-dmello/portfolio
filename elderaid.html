01 · The Problem

Designing for
older adults

This project was created during a fifty-hour design sprint for the IFP UI Challenge. I started by reading about common issues older adults face. Forgetting medication or appointments came up repeatedly, and many healthcare sources point to this as a major cause of preventable health problems.

Older adults often struggle with long or complex tasks because working memory tends to decline with age. This meant the app needed short steps, clear visuals and predictable flows. At the same time, I realised that elderly care is rarely handled by one person. Caregivers and family members also carry stress and uncertainty: they want to know how their loved ones are doing without feeling intrusive.

Core design challenge

The app needed to serve two users at once: the older adult managing their own care, and the caregiver managing from a distance.

02 · The Solution

Shared health view,
calm journeys

This led to the idea of a shared health view. Both seniors and caregivers can check important readings: blood pressure, hydration, oxygen levels and weight, in a simple dashboard. This reduces worry and helps everyone stay on the same page.

From there, I designed short, focused journeys with large tap areas, high contrast text and simple layouts. Each task is broken into steps small enough to complete comfortably without feeling rushed or confused.

Design intention

Designing for calm was as important as designing for clarity. The interface needed to feel like a helping hand, not a tool that demands attention.

03 · Architecture

Shallow navigation,
clear structure

ElderAid is built around a single home screen that branches into health data, medication reminders and upcoming appointments. This keeps navigation shallow and avoids confusion. The onboarding experience introduces each feature slowly, a step-by-step approach known to help older adults feel more confident with new apps.

The interface uses plain language and clear spacing. Buttons are large and easy to tap. Cards highlight key information in a simple, structured format. Notifications are designed to look familiar so users instantly understand their purpose.

Guiding principle

Technology for older adults must feel safe and calm rather than fast or clever. Every small choice, from font size to confirmation wording, carries real weight.

04 · Reflection

What this project
taught me

The sprint timeline made this project challenging but focused. I spent the first part defining the problem and the second part building the interface. The fast pace helped me simplify the experience and keep only what was necessary.

If I revisit this project, I would test the design with older adults to see how they respond to the spacing, icons and colours. I would also explore features like fall alerts or a way for doctors to share notes directly in the app. Most importantly, it reinforced how important accessibility is when building something that becomes part of someone's daily routine.

Key takeaway

Good design for vulnerable users is not about adding features. It is about removing friction at exactly the moments when friction is most harmful.
