01 · Provocation

The real problem
wasn't the modes

Most people don't care whether they take a bus, a metro, or an auto. They care about one thing: getting from A to B, on time, without having to think too hard.

But almost every mobility app is built around modes: one app for ride-hailing, another for transit, a third for bike share, with no continuity between them. The brief for RouteX asked me to connect different modes into a single, seamless end-to-end journey.

The real design problem

Not: how do I surface all available transport modes? But: how do I design a system that treats every journey (including disrupted ones) as one continuous experience that a stressed, distracted user can trust?

This reframe changed every decision I made. It meant the homepage couldn't be a transport picker. The active journey screen couldn't be a map. And a delayed bus couldn't be an alert. It had to become a new plan.

The sections that follow are not a walkthrough of screens. They are an account of the decisions I made, the problems that forced them, and the tradeoffs I accepted along the way.

02 · Mental Model

Three phases,
three personalities

Before a single screen was drawn, I had to understand how users actually think about travel. When I studied how commuters experience a multi-modal journey, a clear three-phase structure emerged, and each phase demands a fundamentally different interface posture.

1
Before

Planning

High consideration

User has time and can evaluate options carefully. Interface should offer clarity and comparison , without overwhelming. The interface wants options, not answers.

2
During

Execution

Low attention

User is moving, stressed, time-pressured. Interface must communicate the single most important thing in under two seconds. No map. No options. One instruction.

3
Crisis

Disruption

Highest stress

User needs answers, not options. The system must make the decision and , not outsource the decision to the user at exactly the wrong moment.

Most transport apps are good at Planning. A few are decent at Execution. Almost none are thoughtful about Disruption.

Designing Disruption as a first-class experience (not an edge case) was the central product bet in RouteX. This framing had direct design consequences. A system that treats all three phases the same, showing everything at all times, would fail users at the exact moment they need it most.

04 · Visual Language

Legibility,
not delight

The visual direction wasn't chosen for aesthetics. It was chosen for readability under pressure. I took deliberate inspiration from transit wayfinding systems, particularly the NYC subway, rather than consumer mobility apps like Citymapper or Google Maps.

Consumer App Aesthetic
Rich maps as primary canvas
Colour = brand expression
Visual complexity signals capability
Optimised for exploration
Transit Wayfinding Aesthetic (Chosen)
Typographic hierarchy as primary canvas
Colour = functional signal only
Visual restraint signals reliability
Optimised for execution

This translated into three specific choices:

White-first for outdoor legibility
Black/white base palette
Transit blue for action only
No colour-coding of transport modes
Why
Colour-coding requires users to learn and remember a legend, adding cognitive load during execution. RouteX treats the journey as a single unit: mode identity is secondary to journey progress.
Tradeoff
The interface loses the expressiveness of mode-specific colour systems (as seen in Citymapper). This is an intentional sacrifice for execution-time clarity.
Phase 01 · Planning

One question,
not a dashboard

The most common mistake in mobility app homescreens is treating them like dashboards: a map, recent trips, saved locations, promotions, suggested destinations. The logic is that showing more helps users find what they need. The reality is that it raises the cognitive entry cost for the most common task: starting a trip.

The homescreen is a single question: Where to?

  • ↳ Pickup defaults to current location (editable but not primary). The user's destination, not starting point, is what matters.
  • ↳ Contextual intelligence strip ('Next bus to Panjim leaves in 6 min') surfaces just-in-time information without making it the hero of the screen.
  • ↳ Saved places render as compact list rows, not cards. Cards imply equal weight and waste vertical space that users must scroll past.
No map on the homescreen
Why
Maps are powerful during execution but expensive during planning. They invite exploration, add visual noise, and create no functional value when the user's only goal is to declare a destination.
Tradeoff
Users who want a map before selecting a destination have no immediate path to it. Acceptable; the map becomes available as soon as journey options are shown.
Planning · Comparison

Reducing
comparison fatigue

Once a destination is entered, the instinct is to show everything: maps, step-by-step breakdowns, real-time vehicle positions, multi-mode icons. At the planning stage, users need to make one comparison: which journey best fits my constraints right now? That requires three data points: time, cost, and transfers. Everything else is noise until they choose.

Journey results: compare, don't explore

  • ↳ Journey tags limited to one per card: Fastest or Cheapest, never both. Multiple tags create ambiguity about which dimension to prioritise.
  • ↳ Maps absent from the list view. They introduce spatial complexity before the user has committed to a route.
  • ↳ Filter tabs (All / Fastest / Cheapest) surface the decision dimension, not the content. Users tell the system their priority, not the other way around.
Maps excluded from the journey list view
Why
Research on decision fatigue shows that adding irrelevant information to a comparison task increases error rates and time-to-decision. Total time and cost are sufficient at this stage.
Tradeoff
Some users may want to see the route shape before committing. Addressed in the journey detail view, which they can access before confirming.
Phase 02 · Execution

Evolved,
not replaced

The interface had to change personality the moment the journey began. Rather than building a separate 'navigation screen,' I evolved the Journey Details view into an execution mode. Same architecture, different hierarchy. The distinction matters: a new screen requires a cognitive reset. An evolved screen carries continuity; the user already knows where they are in the interface.

Core Execution Principle

At any moment, the user should know exactly what they need to do next, and nothing else.

One active instruction, three visual layers

  • ↳ Current action: Large, high-contrast card. Single imperative: 'Board Bus 12'. Non-negotiable visual dominance.
  • ↳ Next action: Visible but muted beneath the current card. Context without distraction. It's coming, but it's not now.
  • ↳ Completed legs: Greyed out and collapsed above. Preserves the user's sense of progress without cluttering the active view.
  • ↳ ETA sits at the top but is not the hero. The next action is. ETA provides context; the action provides instruction.

I deliberately avoided turn-by-turn navigation. Most multi-modal journeys involve significant waiting: at bus stops, on a train, between transfers. Turn-by-turn was designed for continuous movement (driving). Imposing that model on a journey with stops and waits creates visual complexity without useful output.

Execution · Transfer

Reducing anxiety
at the handoff

Transfers are the highest-anxiety moments in a multi-modal journey. The user is leaving one vehicle, navigating an unfamiliar space, and trying not to miss the next connection, often under time pressure.

Transfer guidance: overlay, not screen

  • ↳ One primary instruction only. 'Walk to Platform 3', not a paragraph of wayfinding prose with cardinal directions.
  • ↳ Buffer time shown as a soft chip ('~5 min') rather than a countdown. Countdowns create urgency even when none exists.
  • ↳ The full journey context remains visible underneath. The user always knows where they are in the whole journey. The overlay supplements rather than replaces.
Transfer guidance as overlay, not new screen
Why
A new screen interrupts the journey timeline and requires the user to navigate back. An overlay keeps the full journey context visible; the user can always see where they are in the whole journey.
Tradeoff
Overlays can obscure content behind them. Mitigated by the overlay's limited scope: it only appears for transfers, and dismisses automatically once the user boards.
Phase 03 · Disruption

A disruption is not
an alert

A disruption is a moment of trust. The user is mid-journey, time-pressured, and anxious. What the system does in the next five seconds determines whether the user trusts it or abandons it. Most apps handle disruptions with alerts: banners, notifications, modals. Alerts shift the burden to the user. They say: something changed, figure out what to do.

Core Disruption Principle

A disruption is not an alert. It is a journey state change. The system's job is not to tell the user that something changed. Its job is to have already figured out what to do about it, and to present that decision for confirmation, not deliberation.

One plan. Not a menu.

  • ↳ The original plan is de-emphasised (greyed and struck through) but not hidden. The user can still see what changed and why.
  • ↳ The replacement shows: which vehicle, what time, any cost difference, revised ETA. Everything needed to say yes. Nothing else.
  • ↳ 'See other routes' is available as a secondary action but is visually quiet. It is not the default response to a disruption. It is an escape hatch.
Show one updated plan, not a comparison list
Why
During a disruption, the user is stressed and time-pressured. Presenting multiple alternatives forces them into a decision-making task at exactly the wrong moment. The system should make the decision and ask for confirmation, not outsource it.
Tradeoff
Some scenarios may have a suboptimal recommended plan (e.g. user has mobility constraints, or an unfamiliar route). The 'See other routes' escape hatch addresses this without making it the default path.
De-emphasise the original plan rather than remove it
Why
Hiding the original plan makes it harder for the user to understand what changed. Showing both (with the original visually muted) preserves the comparison while making the recommended path unambiguous.
Tradeoff
Some users may be confused by the old plan still visible. The disruption label ('Bus 12 is delayed') is placed prominently to explain context before they parse the two options.

08 · Scope Decisions

What I left out,
and why

Scope decisions are design decisions. The brief invited exhaustive feature thinking. I made a deliberate choice not to design everything; getting the core journey system right was more valuable than spreading the design thin across every edge case.

Feature
Why I considered it
Why I deprioritised it
Full payment flow
Multi-modal journeys involve complex payment logic: pass coverage, split fares, cash vs. digital.
Payment UX for transit is a project unto itself. Referenced inline ('Pay ₹20 on board') and scoped as a future workstream.
Live vehicle tracking map
Seeing your bus on a map is reassuring during waits between transfers.
Map rendering during execution adds visual noise without changing the user's required action. Glanceability trumped expressiveness.
Social / shared journeys
RouteX's data model supports tracking another person's journey (e.g. a parent tracking a child).
The core single-user journey needed to be solid before introducing a multi-user interaction model.
Accessibility features
RouteX's user base would include people with mobility needs, visual impairments, and cognitive differences.
Noted as a critical future workstream. The visual language (high contrast, large touch targets, plain language) was designed with accessibility as a foundation, not afterthought.

09 · Future Work

What I'd
validate next

RouteX was designed without user testing, a known limitation of any timed challenge. Good design under constraints is not finished design. Below are the three areas I'd prioritise for validation if this moved forward.

01
Disruption UX
The single-plan hypothesis
My hypothesis is that presenting one updated plan reduces stress and increases trust, based on decision fatigue research and cognitive load theory. But individual users have very different preferences for autonomy. Real commuters in real disruption scenarios would be the first thing I'd run.
02
Trigger Logic
Transfer overlay timing
The overlay is designed to appear as the user approaches a transfer. But 'approaching' is ambiguous. Does it appear based on location? Time? The user's declared route? The trigger logic matters as much as the UI itself. I'd prototype and test several trigger conditions before committing to implementation.
03
Onboarding
Execution screen for new users
The execution screen's hierarchy is optimised for users who understand the system. First-time users may not immediately recognise the 'completed above, active prominent, upcoming below' pattern. A short onboarding moment, on the first journey only, might be needed to orient new users without cluttering the main experience.

10 · Final Reflection

"The best design for high-stakes moments is not more information. It is fewer decisions."

RouteX was not complex in terms of feature count. It was complex in terms of context. The same user, on the same journey, needs the interface to behave fundamentally differently depending on whether they're planning, moving, or dealing with a disruption.

The principles I used (journey-first, glanceable, calm over clever) were not aesthetic preferences. They were functional constraints derived from a specific insight about when and how people use transit apps. Every major decision in this project traces back to at least one of them.

What I'm most proud of is not the disruption screen's visual design. It's the reasoning behind it: that a disruption should not increase the user's decision-making burden. That insight, and the willingness to act on it even when it means showing less, is what I hope comes through in this case study.
